## Sources and claim mapping

1. **Dogs Trust — Dog walking problems, pulling on lead and how to help**
   https://www.dogstrust.org.uk/dog-advice/training/unwanted-behaviours/pulling-on-lead
   Recommends a flat fixed-length clip lead and comfortable harness; flexi/bungee leads release length when pulled and can confuse training. Discusses fear, pain, tiredness, refusal, vet assessment and quieter/shorter walks.
2. **RSPCA Australia — What equipment should I use when teaching my dog or puppy to walk on a leash?** (updated 2023-01-10)
   https://kb.rspca.org.au/categories/companion-animals/dogs/training/what-equipment-should-i-use-when-teaching-my-dog-or-puppy-to-walk-on-a-leash
   Supports comfortable correctly sized equipment, harness, approximately 2 m lead; discourages retractable/bungee leads; recommends reward-based loose-lead learning.
3. **Preventive Vet — Are Retractable Leashes Good or Bad?** Cathy Madson (updated 2024-10-03)
   https://www.preventivevet.com/dogs/are-retractable-leashes-good-or-bad
   Explains reel tension and conflict with slack-lead training; acknowledges limited responsible uses, so do not turn the training recommendation into a blanket claim about every use. Commercial/affiliate content; use alongside independent charity guidance, not as sole evidence.
4. **Blue Cross — Life on a lead: keeping walks fun for your dog** (reviewed 2024-09-03)
   https://www.bluecross.org.uk/advice/dog/behaviour-and-training/life-on-a-lead
   Supports sniff walks, safe dog-led exploration, optional “go sniff” cue, slower pace and potentially several minutes at a smell. No universal one-minute limit.
5. **Dogs Trust — Teach your dog to walk nicely on a lead**
   https://www.dogstrust.org.uk/dog-advice/training/outdoors/walking-nicely-training
   Supports easy initial setting, rewarding voluntary proximity/movement, gradual difficulty and calm stopping rather than jerking.
6. **Preventive Vet — Dog Boredom Buster: The Sniffari Walk** (updated 2025-10-17)
   https://www.preventivevet.com/dogs/boredom-busters-for-your-dog-sniffari-walk
   Supports rewarding voluntary check-ins and practised recall during exploration, longer fixed lines only in safe suitable spaces, and distance from people/dogs/roads. The departure-cue sequence below is an editorial synthesis of reward-based skills, not a quoted protocol or diagnosis.

### Description and first-step preparation

**PL:** „Luźna smycz to spacer bez ciągnięcia, nie chodzenie przy nodze. Pies może bezpiecznie węszyć i zmieniać pozycję, o ile smycz pozostaje luźna. Do nauki wybierz zwykłą smycz o stałej długości, około 2 m, oraz wygodne, dobrze dopasowane szelki. Dostosuj długość do otoczenia i wielkości psa. Nie polecamy tu smyczy automatycznej: odblokowany mechanizm utrzymuje napięcie i oddaje kolejną długość, gdy pies ciągnie, co utrudnia czytelne ćwiczenie luźnej smyczy. Zablokowana smycz może mieć luz, ale do tego treningu prostsza jest zwykła smycz.”

**EN:** “Loose-lead walking means walking without pulling, not formal heel. Your dog can safely sniff and change position while the lead stays slack. For training, choose a regular fixed-length lead, around 2 m, and a comfortable, well-fitting harness. Adapt the length to the surroundings and your dog's size. We do not recommend a retractable lead for this exercise: the unlocked reel maintains tension and releases more length when the dog pulls, making the slack-lead lesson less clear. A locked lead can have slack, but a regular lead makes this training simpler.”

First-step setup adds the recommended equipment in both languages. First-step note explains rewarding near the owner during practice without requiring heel throughout the walk. Retain the existing three-step success criterion.

### Step 2 note and step 4 instruction/note

Step 2: calm stopping applies to a comfortable dog pulling toward a goal, not a dog trying to escape danger/fear. In the latter case help the dog move to safety and lower difficulty; do not insist on standing beside the trigger. Remove the claim that waiting is the entire intervention.

Step 4: explicitly pair short training stretches with safe sniffing pauses. Replace “sniff-free walk is punishment” with sniffing as an important natural need and exploration, available outside practice too. Do not make the existing twenty-step criterion a prerequisite for every sniff.

### Troubleshooting: five cards, same order in PL/EN

1. **Pulling throughout the walk / Ciągnie przez cały spacer** (revise): Pulling can be reinforced by reaching goals, but excitement, fear and difficulty also matter. Practise short stretches somewhere quiet, reward slack before it tightens, pause calmly for comfortable goal-directed pulling, and lower difficulty if repeatedly unsuccessful. No “boring walks work” guarantee; no stopping rule that prevents retreat from fear.
2. **Pulling at the start / Ciągnie na początku** (revise): Excitement may contribute; it is not certainly the only cause. Offer calm safe sniffing before short easy practice, without a mandatory minute or guaranteed calming result.
3. **Sniffing at every step / Węszy co krok** (add): This can be normal information gathering. Allow safe exploration and a slower/shorter route. Alternate free sniffing with brief practice; optionally introduce „wąchaj” / “go sniff”. No constant eye-contact requirement or need to earn every sniff.
4. **Hard to move on from a smell / Trudno ruszyć dalej od zapachu** (add): In an easy setting, say „idziemy” / “let's go” once when the dog is already ready to move; reward voluntary attention and a few slack-lead steps. Practise before using the cue around difficult smells. Give time; if the dog cannot respond, lower difficulty instead of repeating or pulling. Do not lure a frightened dog toward a trigger.
5. **Freezing, sitting or refusing to walk / Zastyga, siada albo odmawia spaceru** (add): Unlike relaxed sniffing, refusal can reflect pain, illness, tiredness, equipment discomfort or fear. New, persistent or unexplained refusal, especially limping, pain or lethargy: stop training and contact a vet. Otherwise check harness comfort, paws and weather, give a rest or turn back safely. Fear: create distance, no dragging or food-lured approach; seek a qualified reward-based professional for recurring fear after health assessment.

- RSPCA Australia Knowledgebase, “What are common household dangers for pets?” —
  https://kb.rspca.org.au/categories/companion-animals/household-and-garden-dangers/what-are-common-household-dangers-for-pets
  Supports: many household foods/plants/medicines are toxic and can be fatal; cooked

  batteries, string and magnets can obstruct or burn and warrant immediate veterinary
  contact; suspected ingestion → contact the veterinarian promptly/immediately.
  The claim “do not induce vomiting at home” is NOT used: not supported by the text read.
- RSPCA Australia Knowledgebase, “My puppy is chewing everything, what should I do?” —
  https://kb.rspca.org.au/categories/companion-animals/dogs/behaviour/my-puppy-is-chewing-everything-what-should-i-do
  Supports: chewing is normal; punishment or unexamined interruption creates more
  problems; excessive chewing can reflect an underlying issue including physical health
  problems; choking/tooth fracture/ingestion risks → seek veterinary advice; provide
  safe appropriate chew items with supervision and restrict access to what you do not
  want chewed.
- Dogs Trust, “How to train your dog to leave it” and “How to stop your dog from
  stealing” (URLs above): voluntary trade, rewarding from the other hand, gradual
  floor/low-drop practice, new places and difficulty only while succeeding, no chase/
  scaring, professional help when stealing is severe or items are guarded.
- AVSAB position statements and Dogs Trust puppy socialisation pages (the guide's two
  existing sources) re-read 2026-10-29; unchanged support.
